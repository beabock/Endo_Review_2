Abstract
Rationale: The claim that fungal endophytes are ubiquitous is frequently repeated but lacks a rigorous global synthesis. We argue that understanding the geographic and taxonomic distribution of research effort is essential to evaluate this claim and the reliability of our ecological generalizations. 
Methods: We conducted an abstract-led field audit of 18,750 publications. We harmonized host and fungal taxonomy against the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF) backbone and modeled the relationship between research effort, geography, and national Gross Domestic Product (GDP). 
Key Results: We found that the ubiquity claim appears in 2.7% of abstracts. Research is heavily concentrated in high-GDP, temperate nations. Significant taxonomic gaps exist, particularly for early-diverging plant lineages such as Charophyta and Bryophyta. A country’s GDP was the strongest predictor of its study count. 
Main Conclusion: The current evidence base for endophyte ecology is systematically biased, limiting the scope of global ecological generalizations. We provide a priority roadmap of under-sampled regions and taxa to guide future research toward a more representative and robust global understanding.
Introduction
Fungal endophytes, microorganisms that reside within plant tissues for at least a portion of their lifecycle without causing apparent disease, represent a critical component of terrestrial ecosystems (Petrini, 1991; Stone et al., 2000; Wilson, 1995). Their ecological significance is increasingly recognized, from shaping plant community dynamics to producing a vast arsenal of secondary metabolites with significant potential for medicine and agriculture (Kusari et al., 2012; Rodriguez et al., 2009; Strobel, 2003). A foundational assumption often repeated in the literature is that these fungi are ubiquitous, inhabiting virtually all plants in every biome on Earth (Arnold & Lutzoni, 2007; Rodriguez et al., 2009; Stone et al., 2000; Strobel, 2003). While this claim is appealing in its simplicity, it has been propagated without a rigorous, global-scale evidence synthesis. Our analysis of over 18,000 publications reveals that this ubiquity claim is frequently asserted, appearing in approximately 2.7% of abstracts, which underscores the necessity of testing this assumption against the geographic and taxonomic distribution of actual research efforts.
The conclusions of any ecological study are fundamentally constrained by the distribution of its sampling (Mörsdorf et al., 2015; Soberon & Peterson, 2005). If the evidence base for an entire field is geographically or taxonomically skewed, our understanding of global patterns becomes unreliable (Christie et al., 2021; Hughes et al., 2020; Pyšek et al., 2008). Ecological generalizations about endophyte diversity, host specificity, and biome-level adaptation are vulnerable to such sampling bias (Arnold & Lutzoni, 2007; Davis & Shaw, 2008; U’Ren et al., 2012). Prior syntheses have provided valuable, but spatially or taxonomically limited, snapshots of endophyte distribution (Arnold & Lutzoni, 2007; Cosner et al., 2025; Harrison & Griffin, 2020; Schulz & Boyle, 2005; Stone et al., 2000; U’Ren et al., 2012). However, a comprehensive global analysis that integrates geographic, host, and economic factors has been missing. This gap is particularly critical because we find that over 80% of the primary literature on endophytes remains behind paywalls, making large-scale synthesis largely dependent on abstract-level data mining (Orlando et al., 2018). Because the abstract record is itself a biased sample of the literature, a well-studied region or host group is more likely to appear repeatedly there; by the same logic, absence from abstracts is suggestive of limited attention, but not proof of ecological absence.
This study provides the first integrated, global-scale audit of the fungal endophyte literature, directly evaluating the evidence for the field’s ubiquity claim. We systematically quantify the distribution of research effort to map where our ecological inferences are robust and where they are speculative. Our objectives are to: (1) map the geographic concentration of endophyte studies; (2) assess the distribution of sampling effort across biomes and host plant lineages; (3) determine the relationship between research intensity and national economic capacity (GDP); and (4) produce an actionable roadmap of understudied regions and taxa to guide future research.
Based on these objectives, we predict that: 
-	 (Prediction U) The current, globally aggregated evidence base is insufficient to support an unqualified claim of endophyte ubiquity. 
-	(Prediction A) Research effort is highly clustered geographically, with significant gaps in major regions of the world. 
-	(Prediction B) Sampling is non-randomly distributed across biomes and host lineages, favoring certain well-studied groups. 
-	(Prediction C) The intensity of endophyte research is positively correlated with a country’s GDP, reflecting economic rather than purely biological drivers of sampling.
By testing these predictions, we aim to reframe the conversation from asking if endophytes are ubiquitous to asking where we have sufficient evidence to test ecological and evolutionary hypotheses, thereby providing a data-driven foundation for the next generation of global endophyte research.
Methods
Main Methods
Corpus Assembly and Scope
To construct our evidence base, we consolidated publication records from Web of Science, Scopus, and PubMed using a standardized set of search terms related to fungal endophytes. This workflow utilized two distinct data scales. First, an initial screening corpus of 21,891 unique records was assembled for the purpose of auditing the prevalence of the endophyte ubiquity claim. This broader set was intentionally maintained prior to final relevance filtering to ensure that we captured the propagation of the claim across the wider ecological literature, including studies that may mention endophytes as background context without being primary endophyte research. Second, after applying strict relevance and deduplication filters, we established a final analysis corpus of 18,750 unique publications for the detailed mapping of geographic, taxonomic, and economic biases.
A critical barrier to global synthesis in this field remains the accessibility of primary literature (Cvitanovic et al., 2014; Nuñez et al., 2021; Orlando et al., 2018). Our retrieval process secured open-access full texts for 3,068 records (16.4%) within the final corpus, leaving the majority (n = 15,682) accessible only at the abstract level. We therefore explicitly framed this study as an abstract-led field audit. This approach provides a conservative screen for global research trends, because abstracts, titles, and keywords do not capture the full literature uniformly (Scherer et al., 2018). Major gaps detected in the abstract record therefore suggest underexplored themes in the published literature.
Taxonomic Resolution and Synonym Handling
To ensure robust lineage-level counts across decades of research, we developed a reproducible pipeline to harmonize plant and fungal taxonomy. All extracted plant and fungal names were processed against the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF) backbone taxonomy (GBIF Secretariat, 2023). Our custom scripts algorithmically mapped and resolved obsolete or changing nomenclature to their currently accepted valid taxonomies. This process reconciled historical taxonomic shifts, such as changes in fungal phyla, ensuring that our analysis of biological coverage was based on a consistent and contemporary taxonomic framework. 
Core Analyses
We performed a series of analyses to quantify sampling bias. First, we assessed geographic concentration by mapping study counts to countries to identify research hotspots. Second, we analyzed the relationship between research effort and economic capacity by correlating per-country study counts with national Gross Domestic Product (GDP). Third, we quantified host taxonomic coverage by summarizing study counts across plant families and orders, which allowed us to identify well-studied and understudied lineages. Fourth, we categorized the types of interactions reported (e.g., endophytic, pathogenic, mutualistic) to understand the functional focus of the literature. Finally, to directly test a central assumption in the field, we developed a large language model workflow to screen the entire corpus for explicit and implicit claims of endophyte ubiquity.
Reproducibility and Validation
All data preprocessing, harmonization, and statistical analyses were performed programmatically to ensure full reproducibility. We developed a highly structured, version-controlled pipeline using both R (version 4.3.0) and Python (version 3.13.5) development environments within Visual Studio Code (VSCode)(Microsoft, 2026; Python Software Foundation, 2025; R Core Team, 2023). 
All custom scripts used in this pipeline have been documented and are available to facilitate reproducing these analyses and figures in a Github repository: https://github.com/beabock/Endo-Review. 
Further details about the methods are included in the Supplementary Methods section.
Supplementary Methods
1. Corpus Assembly and Data Extraction
We compiled the initial corpus using defined search strings across Web of Science, Scopus, and PubMed, with specific export rules to capture bibliographic data and abstracts. The raw exports were consolidated and deduplicated using `api_pull_abstracts.R` and `Combo_abstracts_pull2.R`. Initial data cleaning and formatting were performed with `01_csv_cleanup.py`. We then merged the cleaned files into a final dataset using `merge_final_data.py`. For the subset of records with open-access permissions, we downloaded available PDFs using `download_pdfs.py`. Finally, we used a custom large language model pipeline, `monsoon_extract.py`, to extract structured metadata (e.g., host names, fungal taxa, geography, tissue) from all abstracts and full texts.
We compiled the initial corpus by querying Web of Science, Scopus, and PubMed using a standardized, boolean search string:
  ("fungal endophyte" OR "fungal endophytes" OR "endophytic fungus" OR "endophytic fungi" OR  "latent fungus" OR "latent fungi" OR "systemic fungus" OR "systemic fungi" OR   "internal fungi" OR "resident fungi" OR "seed-borne fungi" OR "seed-transmitted fungi" OR "dark septate endophyte" OR "dark septate fungi" OR "DSE fungi")  AND
 (plant* OR moss* OR bryophyte* OR liverwort* OR hornwort* OR fern* OR lycophyte* OR   pteridophyte* OR tree* OR shrub* OR grass* OR "graminoid*" OR herb* OR   crop* OR seedling* OR sapling* OR seed* OR root* OR leaf* OR foliage OR shoot* OR  stem* OR twig* OR rhizome* OR thallus OR frond* OR algae OR "green alga*" OR macroalga* OR   cyanobacteria OR cyanobiont* OR photobiont* OR lichen*)
The query was designed to capture a broad range of modern and historic fungal endophyte terminology (e.g., endophyte, latent fungus, dark septate endophyte) cross-referenced against diverse plant host classifications (e.g., angiosperms, gymnosperms, bryophytes, charophytes, agricultural crops) and limited to "Journal Article" document types. To manage API limits, data was requested iteratively in six publication year bins spanning from 1700 to 2025 (`api_pull_abstracts.R`, using rentrez, rscopus, and wosr)(Baker, 2018; Muschelli, 2025; Winter, 2017).
The raw data exports were standardized and consolidated using `Combo_abstracts_pull2.R` and `01_csv_cleanup.py`. Given the multi-database approach, we implemented a rigorous three-stage deduplication pipeline: (1) exact-match deduplication by Digital Object Identifier (DOI); (2) text-based deduplication comparing normalized abstract strings (lowercased, collapsed whitespace, stripped punctuation) for records lacking DOIs; and (3) a final document-type filter to remove erroneous non-primary literature. The deduplicated dataset was assembled into a final global metadata file using `merge_final_data.py`.
To move beyond abstract-only limitations where possible, we systematically queried the Unpaywall API for all valid DOIs in our dataset using `download_pdfs.py`(Piwowar et al., 2018; Priem et al., 2016). This step successfully located and downloaded the best available Open Access PDF for 3,068 records (16.4% of the corpus).
For automated data extraction, we developed a highly structured Large Language Model (LLM) pipeline (`monsoon_extract.py`) deployed on the Northern Arizona University Monsoon high-performance computing cluster. The pipeline processed the text from the abstracts and the first 12 pages of the full-text PDFs (extracted via PyMuPDF)(Artifex Software, 2026). We prompted an open-weights model (Mistral7B via the Ollama framework) to act as an expert mycologist, extracting every unique plant-host and fungal-taxon interaction pair into a strictly formatted JSON array (Jiang et al., 2023) . The model simultaneously coded context for each interaction, including the sampled tissue, geographic country, biome, and assumed primary trophic guild. Custom Python logic flattened nested LLM outputs and separated comma-delimited taxa strings (e.g., multiple hosts targeted by a single fungus) prior to appending them being stored into a structured tabularized into our analysis dataset.
2. Taxonomic Resolution and Synonym Handling
To ensure robust lineage-level counts across decades of research, we processed all plant and fungal names extracted by the LLM through a centralized, automated taxonomic standardization pipeline. The core workflow (`taxa_synonym_resolution.py`) algorithmically matched raw names against the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF) backbone taxonomy (GBIF Secretariat, 2023). Because the taxonomic bounds and names of fungi and plants frequently shift (such as corrections to historical morphology-based misclassifications), this standardization ensured all taxa were evaluated using a contemporary and consistent framework.
The resolution utilized multiple algorithmic strategies to maximize matches:
1.	Normalization & Pre-filtering: Raw tokens were text-normalized (lowercased, punctuation stripped, Unicode equivalents resolved) and filtered against a centralized dictionary (`taxon_mapping_config.json` via `taxon_mapping.py`) that categorized nulls (e.g., "unknown", "sp."), generic non-taxon phrases (e.g., "mycorrhizal fungi", "host plants"), and off-target outgroups (e.g., bacteria, oomycetes) which were explicitly excluded from alignment.
2.	Alias Mapping: Known idiosyncratic abbreviations or historically problematic names from the endophyte literature were overridden securely using predefined alias-to-canonical dictionaries before API submission.
3.	GBIF Querying: The normalized names were queried against the GBIF backbone. The pipeline matched accepted canonical names, resolved objective synonyms to their currently accepted equivalent, and accepted fuzzy matches only if they met a high-confidence threshold on exact rank alignments.
4.	Taxonomic Backfilling: For successfully resolved taxa, the script populated higher taxonomic ranks (Kingdom, Phylum, Class, Order, Family, Genus), allowing us to map research effort across missing or historically fractured branches of the Tree of Life.
Any remaining names that failed automated resolution (e.g., highly ambiguous novel operational taxonomic units or poorly digitized culture names) were flagged in the pipeline outputs and subsequently underwent manual review.
3. Metadata Extraction and Standardization
Following data extraction, all metadata fields underwent systematic normalization to prepare the dataset for quantitative synthesis. The main Python script (01_standardize_metadata.py) first normalized extracted string values (e.g., lowercasing, stripping punctuation, resolving unicode limits) and mapped idiosyncratic ecological terminology to standardized categories. For instance, diverse plant anatomical terms (e.g., "culm", "shoot", "xylem") were algorithmically mapped to parent tissue categories (e.g., "stem"), and fine-scale habitat descriptors were binned into a structured vocabulary of primary terrestrial biomes (e.g., translating "farmland" and "orchard" to "agriculture"). The script also implemented aggressive rule-sets to remove technical extraction artifacts and null responses (e.g., "not specified in text", "n/a").
To control for temporal trends in research effort, each record was cross-referenced to extract the original publication year (01a_enrich_publication_year.py). The dataset was then aggressively filtered (02_dataset_filtering.py) to drop irresolvable records or studies confirmed to fall outside the scope of the synthesis (e.g., non-target interactions involving bacteria or non-plant hosts).
Geographic standardization was implemented natively in R to interface with shapefile databases (03_geographic_standardizing.R). A centralized utility mapping nearly 1,000 spelling variants and regional aliases was applied to harmonize country names into standardized ISO 3166-1 alpha-3 codes (country_mapping.R and country_mapping.py) (Codes for the Representation of Names of Countries and Their Subdivisions — Part 1: Country Codes, 2020). The pipeline explicitly stripped broad regional descriptors that lacked country-level resolution (e.g., "Middle East", "Tropics", "South America"). To ensure geospatial consistency, disputed territories and overseas dependencies were programmatically assigned to their parent or administrative ISO codes where appropriate using disputed_territory_parent_iso.R (e.g., assigning records from French Guiana to France for socio-economic linking).
Finally, we utilized 04_country_enrichment.R to bind coordinate centroids (latitude and longitude) and relevant macroeconomic metrics to each harmonized ISO code. Gross Domestic Product (GDP) values for the year 2024 were sourced from the World Bank to map the relationship between national economic capacity and research effort (World Bank, 2024). Final pipeline checkpoints (05_dataset_summary.py and 06_preanalysis_check.py) were run to assert dataset integrity by validating column requirements and tabulating final sample sizes prior to core statistical analyses.
4. Relationship and Bias Classification Rules
To assess potential biases in the functional or applied focus of the field, we classified the reported ecological relationship between each fungus-host pair. Initial classifications were performed during the Large Language Model (LLM) data extraction phase. The model was prompted to infer the primary guild of the fungus based on the context of the abstract or full-text, selecting from a constrained list (Endophytic, Pathogenic, Mutualistic, Saprotrophic, Mycorrhizal, or Unknown).
Because LLM outputs still exhibited idiosyncratic phrasing across tens of thousands of records, we implemented a deterministic normalization dictionary in 01_standardize_metadata.py. This script mapped raw terminology variants to a standardized set of core functional guilds:
-	Pathogen: Mapped from terms like "phytopathogen", "pathogenic", etc.
-	Endophyte: Mapped from "endophyte", "endophytic", "latent fungus", etc.
-	Mycorrhiza: Mapped from "mycorrhizal", "ectomycorrhiza", etc.
-	Biocontrol/PGPR: Applied contexts mapped from terms like "biological control agent", "antagonist", "nematophagous", "plant growth promoting", etc.
-	Mutualist/Symbiont: Mapped from generic beneficial synonyms lacking further specificity.
-	Saprotroph: Mapped from "saprobic", "decomposer", etc.
After standardization, we deployed 07_relationship_type_summary.R to compute aggregate counts of these interaction types by study, country, and year. To determine whether the global evidence base is disproportionately skewed toward applied economics (e.g., disease mitigation or crop yields) rather than fundamental ecology, we cross-referenced these functional guilds against fungal taxonomy and geographic metadata using 04_interaction_bias.R and 03_fungal_taxonomic_bias.py. This allowed us to report the relative weighting of pathogenic, biocontrol, and endophytic interactions across the corpus.
5. Biodiversity Priority Overlap Analysis
To evaluate whether sampling efforts in fungal endophyte research spatially align with global conservation needs, we intersected geographic study frequencies with established national biodiversity priority metrics. Data on endemic species richness and threatened species probabilities were aggregated natively from World Bank and integrated International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) indicator datasets (International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN), 2026; Ooms, 2024; World Bank, 2026). Rather than relying on simplistic binary hotspot delineations, we utilized continuous numeric predictors to provide higher statistical sensitivity during modeling.
The initial intersections and spatial overlaps were generated using an R pipeline (08_biodiversity_priority_overlap.R), which cross-referenced national study counts against priority indicators while explicitly normalizing for national land area. To statistically validate the relationship between global study effort and actual biodiversity value, we utilized a dedicated Python toolkit (09_biodiversity_priority_robustness.py) to systematically test for sampling unevenness. These validation steps included Spearman rank correlations comparing baseline study counts against priority metrics, as well as Kruskal-Wallis tests to assess the variance of study distribution across distinct biodiversity quartiles. Furthermore, we conducted chi-square tests of independence to determine whether a country's status as understudied diverged significantly from its expected status as a high-priority biodiversity zone. Finally, sensitivity analyses utilizing varied quantile thresholds, regional subsampling by continent, and direct correlations against Gross Domestic Product (GDP) were employed to disentangle whether observed geographical research biases are driven primarily by actual biodiversity density or by underlying regional economic disparities.
6. Ubiquity Claim Detection and Full-Text Screening
To systematically evaluate the prevalence of the assertion that fungal endophytes are "ubiquitous" or universally distributed across all plant species, we conducted an automated text-mining analysis utilizing a Large Language Model, Mistral 7B (Jiang et al., 2023). The corpus for this step consisted of full-text documents (PDF, TXT, and Markdown formats) alongside parsed abstracts. Full-text PDFs were scraped using a custom Python pipeline (06_detect_ubiquity_claims_ollama.py), which compressed whitespace and extracted up to the first 8,000 characters of each document. This length threshold successfully captured the title, abstract, and introductory paragraphs, which is where foundational background claims regarding global distribution are typically located.
Due to the computational intensity of scanning thousands of full-text documents, inference was executed on the Northern Arizona University Monsoon high-performance computing cluster. The task was subdivided into discrete parallel shards (submit_ubiquity_shards.sh) and managed via SLURM job scheduling (run_06_ubiquity_claims_ollama.sbatch), querying a local, GPU-accelerated Ollama server to ensure secure and rapid processing without external API limits.
The LLM was prompted to act as an information extraction model for scientific literature, with explicit instructions to determine whether the provided text contained a claim that fungal endophytes are "ubiquitous, near-universal, or found in nearly all plants." To guarantee programmatic reproducibility, the model was constrained to output responses in a strict JSON format. The required schema encoded a boolean classification flag, an evaluation of the claim's strength (explicit, qualified, or none), the taxonomic scope of the claim, the model's confidence score, and the exact extracted evidence sentences. Furthermore, the model was instructed to extract the specific citations attached to these claims, allowing us to trace the root literature underpinning the ubiquity paradigm. Following inference, the independent output shards were aggregated via a dedicated merge protocol (merge_ubiquity_shards.py) to produce the final dataset of categorical dataset utilized in the manuscript's discussion.
7. Validation 
To ensure the trustworthiness of our automated pipeline, we implemented a structured validation approach covering data parsing, taxonomic resolution, and downstream spatial analytics. Prior to statistical modeling, the dataset was strictly filtered using 02_dataset_filtering.py, which systematically removed irrelevant interactions, non-target ecological guilds, and unsupported phylogenetic lineages. The integrity of this filtered dataset was subsequently verified using a dedicated QA audit (06_preanalysis_check.py) to confirm the successful capture of valid geographic and interaction pairings. During the taxonomic normalization phase, any organism string that the computational backbones could not reliably match to a known, valid lineage was exported as an independent audit file. These ambiguous taxonomic entries underwent qualitative human-in-the-loop review to resolve orthographic errors and actively prevent language model hallucinations from entering the baseline taxonomy. The sanitized datasets were subsequently analyzed in 02_taxonomy.R, while entirely unstudied and understudied host lineages were explicitly isolated and quantified using 03_identify_understudied_taxa.R.
8. Figure Generation and Presentation
All statistical data visualizations and geographic projections were generated natively within the R programming environment utilizing the ggplot2 package (Wickham, 2016). To construct the global study-density maps, national boundary multipolygons were sourced programmatically using rnaturalearth and sf (simple features), and spatial layers were projected to visualize cumulative geographic sampling efforts (geographic_plotting.R) (Massicotte & South, 2026; Pebesma, 2018). To accommodate the extreme positive right-skew inherent to publication densities (i.e., a small number of countries host the vast majority of studies), study counts were log10-transformed prior to mapping. Visualizations for taxonomic saturation (taxonomy_representation.R) and ecological relationship frequencies (relationship_type_plots.R) were primarily generated as percentage-coverage bar charts, temporal trend-lines, and cross-tabulation heatmaps (tidyr, reshape2)(Wickham, 2007; Wickham et al., 2014). To ensure accessibility and strict aesthetic consistency across the entirety of the manuscript, a custom stylistic and typographic configuration (theme_utils.R) was sourced globally by all plotting scripts. All categorical visualizations strictly utilized a high-contrast, colorblind-safe color palette (inspired by the Okabe-Ito spectrum), while spatial maps adopted sequential, color-scaled gradients to accurately model data density variances without perceptual artifacts (Okabe & Ito, 2008).
9. Reproducibility
The computational pipeline was partitioned into local and high-performance computing (HPC) workflows based on hardware demands.
Local Processing: Lightweight computational processes, such as the initial bibliometric dataset cleanup (01_csv_cleanup.py), metadata standardization (01_standardize_metadata.py), taxonomic harmonization (taxa_synonym_resolution.py), and aggregate summary statistics, were processed locally. This local pipeline phase included the execution of all spatial mapping and aesthetic plotting routines (e.g., geographic_plotting.R, biome_plots.R), as well as the polynomial regression modeling used to analyze global Gross Domestic Product (GDP) and latitudinal trends (01_country_gdp_latitude_analysis.R).
High-Performance Computing (Monsoon): The computationally intensive Natural Language Processing (NLP) components of the study, specifically the foundational extraction of metadata (monsoon_extract.py) and the subsequent detection of global ubiquity claims (06_detect_ubiquity_claims_ollama.py), were executed on Northern Arizona University's Monsoon HPC cluster. These text-mining analyses leveraged a self-hosted Large Language Model (Ollama; Llama3-8b) and were parallelized across sharded dataset chunks utilizing SLURM job scheduling arrays (e.g., run_06_ubiquity_claims_ollama.sbatch). To guarantee secure and continuous processing without external API throttling, individual SLURM tasks were provisioned with 1 node, 4 CPUs, 16 GB of memory, and 1 GPU. This hardware configuration ensured robust throughput for iterating over the final corpus of 18,750 abstracts and 3,068 full texts
Results
Prediction U: The Evidence Base for Evaluating Endophyte Ubiquity
To evaluate the prevalence of the ubiquity claim, we screened the available texts of the initial screening corpus (n = 21,891). Our large language model pipeline identified 586 positive ubiquity claims, representing 2.7% of this broader corpus. These results comprised 334 explicit statements and 252 qualified ones. This finding supports the assumption outlined in Prediction U, demonstrating that while the ubiquity claim is a recurring theme across the literature, the globally aggregated evidence base is heavily constrained. This indicates that the assumption of ubiquity is frequently asserted without a high-resolution audit of the sampling evidence. Our analysis of the final analysis corpus (n = 18,750) reveals that of these unique publications, we could only access the full text for 3,068 records (16.4%), with the remaining 15,682 records analyzed at the abstract level. The following results present our audit of this evidence base, structured to test the remaining predictions regarding sampling bias.

Prediction A: Research Effort is Geographically Clustered
Supporting our first prediction (Prediction A), we found that fungal endophyte research is strongly concentrated in a small number of countries (Figure 1). Of the 234 countries and territories analyzed, 80 had no recorded studies in our corpus. The majority of research was concentrated in North America, Europe, and East Asia, leaving vast regions of Africa, Central Asia, and South America with little to no evidence of sampling. It is important to note that abstracts often omit precise location metadata, meaning a zero count for a country is not definitive proof of sampling absence but rather a strong indicator of its underrepresentation in the global literature. Our analysis reveals a striking geographic concentration of research effort. A small number of countries account for the vast majority of studies in our corpus. Specifically, studies from China (1,600 studies), the United States (909 studies), and India (709 studies) collectively represent a substantial portion of the global endophyte literature, while most of the world's nations remain dramatically under-represented.

Prediction B: Biome and Host Representation are Non-Random
Consistent with Prediction B, sampling effort was not only geographically clustered but also highly skewed across biomes and host taxa. Within and across countries, research was disproportionately focused on temperate forests and agricultural biomes, while biomes such as tropical and subtropical grasslands, savannas, and shrublands were under-sampled (Figure 2). This finding carries the caveat that biome-level information is often missing from abstracts, so our analysis likely underestimates the full extent of this bias.
 
Figure 2. Heatmap of study counts by biome and country. This figure reveals that research effort is not only geographically clustered but also heavily skewed toward certain biomes. Temperate forests and agricultural systems are overrepresented in the literature, while biomes such as tropical and subtropical grasslands, savannas, and shrublands are significantly underrepresented, even within well-studied countries.
Host taxonomic representation was similarly imbalanced (Figure 3). A small number of plant families, notably Poaceae and Fabaceae, have received the vast majority of research attention. Our analysis, which standardized host taxonomy against the GBIF backbone (GBIF Secretariat, 2023), revealed that numerous plant orders and families have no recorded endophyte studies, representing major gaps in our knowledge of host-fungus interactions. A strong taxonomic bias is also evident in the choice of host plants. The research landscape is dominated by a few economically important agricultural grass species. The top four most-studied host plants—Lolium arundinaceum (Tall Fescue), Oryza sativa (Rice), Zea mays (Corn), and Lolium perenne (Perennial Ryegrass)—all belong to the family Poaceae. This narrow focus on agricultural systems leaves the vast majority of plant diversity unsampled. 
This pattern of narrow focus extends to the fungal partners. The most frequently studied fungal genera are those with well-known roles as agricultural pathogens, plant mutualists, or human health concerns. Genera such as Fusarium, Aspergillus, and the grass symbiont Epichloe dominate the literature, while the broader diversity of fungal life remains largely unexplored in the context of endophytism.

Research is heavily biased toward economically important families such as Poaceae and Fabaceae, while numerous other plant orders and families, including early-diverging lineages like Charophyta and Bryophyta, have no recorded endophyte studies.
Prediction C: Study Intensity Increases with GDP
In line with Prediction C, we found a strong, positive correlation between a country's economic output and its endophyte research intensity. A country's log-transformed Gross Domestic Product (GDP) was a significant predictor of its log-transformed study count (Spearman's ρ = 0.83, p < 0.001), indicating that wealthier nations are studied more frequently (Figure 4). Further multiple regression modeling revealed that GDP was a vastly superior predictor of study count compared to ecological metrics. When modeled together, the predictive coefficient for economic status (log10 GDP: ~0.56, p < 0.001) overwhelmingly dominated the predictive effect of raw biodiversity (total species richness: < 0.001, p = 0.003). Likewise, independent non-parametric assessments confirmed that economic output (Spearman's ρ = 0.83) correlates much more robustly with sampling intensity than does absolute species richness (Spearman's ρ = 0.58). This indicates that the global distribution of endophyte research is more a map of economic capacity than it is a map of biodiversity. This creates a critical knowledge gap, as high-biodiversity, low-GDP countries are systematically under-represented in the current body of endophyte research.

Priority Outputs for Future Sampling
Based on these identified gaps, our pipeline generated priority lists of understudied countries and host taxa. The results highlight numerous plant genera and families with no representation in the literature, including early-diverging lineages such as Charophyta and Bryophyta. Likewise, many countries—including Angola, Armenia, and the Bahamas—remain completely unsampled. These outputs provide a concrete roadmap for targeted sampling efforts designed to fill the most significant gaps in our current understanding of global endophyte diversity. We found a significant disconnect between research effort and global biodiversity patterns. Numerous countries recognized for their high biodiversity value have received no attention in the endophyte literature. For example, countries with high biodiversity priority scores, such as Belize, remain completely un-sampled in our dataset. More strikingly, microstates with exceptionally high metrics for species density and endemism, such as Liechtenstein and Monaco, are also entirely absent from the literature, representing critical blind spots in our understanding of global endophyte distribution.
Discussion
Our analysis of the global endophyte literature reveals a research landscape that is geographically and taxonomically constrained. The patterns of data availability are not random; instead, they are strongly shaped by economic factors that are decoupled from the planet’s biological richness. This structure has profound implications for how we interpret the existing evidence for endophyte ubiquity and for how we should direct future research.
Interpreting the Evidence Gaps
A primary finding of our study is the large number of countries and biomes with zero recorded endophyte studies in our abstract-based corpus. We interpret these zeros not as a definitive statement of endophyte absence, but as thematic blind spots in the global research effort. The lack of accessible evidence from these regions prevents the formation of credible ecological generalizations. This situation is analogous to the “file drawer problem” in meta-analysis, where unpublished, non-significant results can skew conclusions (Rosenthal, 1979). Here, the absence of data from entire regions creates a systematic bias, limiting our ability to construct a truly global view of endophyte ecology. These gaps highlight areas where our collective knowledge is too sparse to support broad claims about fungal distribution and function.
Ecological Implications of Sampling Bias
The concentration of research in temperate, high-GDP nations has significant ecological consequences. Fungal biogeography is known to be structured by strong environmental gradients and historical factors (Tedersoo et al., 2014). By over-sampling a narrow range of temperate and agricultural systems, the field risks inflating its confidence in patterns of endophyte diversity, host affinity, and ecological function that may not hold true in other contexts. For example, conclusions about community assembly or transmission modes derived predominantly from North American and European forests may be poor predictors of the dynamics in under-sampled tropical systems, which harbor a vastly different suite of host plants and environmental pressures. This bias represents a lost opportunity to understand the full spectrum of endophyte symbioses and their roles in ecosystem processes across the globe.
Economic Privilege as a Driver of Research Effort
Our results demonstrate that research effort follows economic privilege rather than biological richness. The strong positive correlation between a country’s GDP and its study count indicates that the current map of endophyte knowledge is more a reflection of funding and infrastructure than of a strategic effort to explore global biodiversity. This economic filtering of research focus means we are likely missing significant fungal novelty from the world’s most biodiverse, and often least wealthy, regions. This is a critical lost opportunity, as these under-explored ecosystems may harbor unique fungal lineages with novel ecological functions and potential for bioprospecting (Bertini et al., 2022; Newman & Cragg, 2012).
The Utility and Limitations of an Abstract-Based Audit
We acknowledge that primarily mining abstracts is a limitation of this study. This approach cannot capture every mention of an endophyte in the full text of a publication. However, we argue that this method serves as a necessary and conservative field audit of the most visible and accessible evidence. If a study’s core findings on endophyte presence are not mentioned in the abstract, their utility for broad-scale synthesis is inherently limited. Furthermore, this approach highlights the systematic barriers created by paywalled science; by relying on the most commonly accessible part of a publication, our analysis maps the boundaries of our default knowledge, which is shaped by what is readily available to the global research community.
A Roadmap for Future Sampling
Based on these findings, we propose a strategic roadmap for future endophyte research. To build a more robust and representative understanding of endophyte ecology, sampling efforts should be intentionally directed toward two key areas. First, there is a critical need to explore host lineages that are currently underrepresented in the literature. Our analysis shows a heavy concentration on economically important plant families. A concerted effort to sample early-diverging and non-vascular plant lineages, such as Charophyta and Bryophyta, would provide crucial insights into the evolutionary history of endophytism. Second, research must expand into high-biodiversity, low-GDP regions. Countries such as Tonga and large portions of Africa and Central Asia represent significant gaps in our knowledge. A targeted, collaborative effort to sample in these regions is essential for discovering novel fungal diversity and for testing the generality of ecological theories developed in temperate zones. Such a program would not only advance the field of endophyte ecology but also contribute to global biodiversity conservation goals.
